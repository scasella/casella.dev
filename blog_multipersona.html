Panel-style reasoning trades accuracy for shorter completions

[ 2026-04-24 · updated 2026-09-23 ] // Qwen3-30B-A3B // 7 MIN READ

On MATH500 level 5 and AIME, a small adapter on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base that reasons as a panel of experts was less accurate than Qwen's production thinking model, and its completions were several times shorter.

GitHub repo LoRA adapter
TL;DR

I trained a LoRA adapter on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base to reason as a panel of experts and compared it with Qwen's production thinking model. On matched samples, the panel was 31.5 points less accurate on MATH500 level 5 (318 against 487 correct of 536 matched samples: 59.3% against 90.9%) and 50.6 points less accurate on AIME 2024–2025 (22.5% against 73.1%). Per correct sampled answer, the thinking model used 5.07× as many completion tokens on MATH500 level 5 and 2.48× on AIME.

The two models differ in post-training as well as in reasoning format, so these results describe the two models, not the effect of the format alone.

What was compared

The panel model is a rank-32 LoRA adapter on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Base, a mixture-of-experts model with 30 billion parameters, about 3 billion active per token. I trained it with reinforcement learning only, with no example answers or distillation. The prompt asks for a debate inside one tag and a final answer inside another. The reward is answer correctness, minus 0.2 when the tags are missing or malformed. Training had two stages: 128 steps on GSM8K, grade-school math word problems, then 128 steps on MATH. Each step used 16 problems with 8 samples per problem, at learning rate 5×10⁻⁶. 

# Panel prompt template (used at both train and eval time)
A conversation between User and Multi-Persona Panel of Experts.
The user asks a question, and the Panel solves it. The Panel first
deliberates and debates the reasoning process with each other and
then provides the user with the answer.

User: {problem}
Assistant: <mutipersonaDebate> … </mutipersonaDebate> <answer> … </answer>

The comparison model is Qwen/Qwen3-30B-A3B, Qwen's production hybrid model, run with apply_chat_template(enable_thinking=True) so that it writes a <think> block before answering. It has the same architecture but went through Qwen's full post-training, not my two RL stages.

Both models were sampled at temperature 1.0 and graded by the same answer checker. Token limits and sample counts were not the same:

Accuracy and completion length

Token counts are completion tokens as the sampler recorded them. Tokens per correct sampled answer is the total over a set of samples divided by the number that were correct. Table 02 computes both accuracy and tokens on one set of matched samples: each thinking sample paired with the panel sample of the same index on the same problem. That gives 536 pairs on MATH500 level 5 (the panel's first 4 of 8 samples per problem) and 320 on AIME.

Table 02Accuracy and completion tokens per correct answer, matched samples
Problem set (pairs) Panel accuracy Thinking accuracy Panel tokens / correct Thinking tokens / correct Thinking ÷ panel
MATH500 level 5 (536) 59.3% 90.9% 1,652 8,376 5.07×
AIME 2024 + 2025 (320) 22.5% 73.1% 6,257 15,491 2.48×

The panel was right on 318 of 536 MATH500 pairs and the thinking model on 487; on AIME, 72 and 234 of 320. All-sample pass@1 differs only on MATH500 level 5, where it adds the panel's other 4 samples per problem: 58.4% against 90.9%, a 32.5-point gap. On AIME every sample is in the matched set.

On AIME the mean completion was 1,408 tokens for the panel and 11,328 for the thinking model, about one-eighth as many.

Token limits affect tokens per correct

Many thinking samples that ended wrong stopped at the 16,384-token limit: the median is 16,384 tokens for pairs where neither model was right, on both sets. Those cut-off samples add tokens and no correct answers, which raises the thinking model's tokens per correct. The both-correct medians (4,958 and 5,510) are well below the limit, so the 5.89× to 6.91× length ratios come from finished answers.

Pass@k

Pass@k, the chance that at least one of k samples is correct, uses all samples. The panel is below the thinking model at every k I measured, and the gap narrows as k grows.

Table 04Pass@k gap (panel minus thinking) on three problem sets
Problem set Range of k Gap at k=1 Gap at the largest k Change
MATH500 L1 to L5 1 to 4 −16.5 points −10.0 points +6.5 points
MATH500 level 5, all 1 to 4 −32.5 points −17.8 points +14.7 points
AIME 2024+2025 1 to 16 −50.6 points −35.0 points +15.6 points

A narrowing pass@k gap can arise under independent sampling without any difference in search strategy, so the narrowing here does not by itself identify a diversity mechanism. For example, on a problem the panel solves half the time and the thinking model 90% of the time, the gap is 0.40 at k = 1 and about 0.06 at k = 4 (0.9375 against 0.9999). On the 50-problem MATH500 set the thinking model also reaches the ceiling: its pass@3 is 1.0, so from k = 3 the gap can only shrink. On AIME, the panel solved 11 of the 20 problems at least once in 16 samples and the thinking model 18, and no problem was solved only by the panel.

Interpretation and limits

On these problem sets, the panel adapter is less accurate per sample than Qwen's thinking model and uses fewer completion tokens per correct sampled answer. The comparison does not isolate the reasoning format. The panel is my base-model LoRA after 256 RL steps; the thinking model went through Qwen's full post-training. Either difference could produce the accuracy and length gaps. Separating them takes the matched-format control below.

Three things affect the estimates themselves:

  • Token limits and sample counts differ (Table 01). Truncated thinking samples raise its tokens per correct, and I did not count how many panel samples hit their lower limits.
  • The sets are small: 20 AIME problems, 134 MATH500 level-5 problems and a 50-problem MATH500 slice.
  • Each training stage is a single run with one seed.

Next experiment

  • A matched-format control: a thinking-format arm trained from the same base with the same recipe (RL only, same reward, 128 GSM8K and 128 MATH steps, same LoRA rank), with both arms evaluated at the same token limit and sample count.
  • Count how many panel samples stop at their token limit, and extend pass@k beyond 16 samples.
  • Rerun the appendix's dispersion measure with persona names stripped, on fixed-length chunks of the full trace, and add a blinded annotation that counts distinct solution approaches per problem.

Appendix: between-trace embedding dispersion

This exploratory measure asks how different a model's samples on one problem are from each other. I embedded the reasoning text inside the debate or think tag with all-mpnet-base-v2, a standard sentence-embedding model, and averaged the cosine distance over all pairs of samples from the same model on each problem. A paired t-test over problems compares the two models.

Table A1Between-trace embedding dispersion, same problems
Problem set Panel Thinking Difference t df
MATH500 L1 to L5 (50 problems, 4 samples each) 0.095 0.053 +0.042 (+78%) 5.91 49
AIME 2024+2025 (20 problems, 16 samples each) 0.119 0.068 +0.051 (+76%) 4.72 19

Three things limit what this number can mean:

  • It sees only the opening. The embedding model reads about 384 word pieces and drops the rest. Most thinking traces, and many panel traces, are longer.
  • The persona names are included. Panel samples name their speakers differently ("Expert 1", "Persona 2", invented names). Part of the distance can come from names, phrasing and formatting rather than from different strategies.
  • It compares samples within one model. It does not show whether the panel reaches ideas the thinking model does not.

The MATH training stage lowered the panel's dispersion on the same 50 problems from 0.174 to 0.095 (t = −7.95, df = 49). At the clustering threshold I used (DBSCAN, eps 0.25), almost every problem's samples formed a single cluster for both models.
