A 50-step RL update reduces categorical sampling bias

[ 2026-05-26 · updated 2026-09-23 ] // Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct // 7 MIN READ

Training on one random-integer task flattened measured answer distributions on nine others. Small capability checks showed no observed decline.

GitHub repo LoRA adapter
What this measures

Each task gives the model a fixed list of valid answers, such as 20 colors. Candidate scoring, used for the main chart and table, takes the model's log-probability of every candidate, normalizes over the list and reports how far that distribution is from uniform. Sampled results generate answers and count them. Each figure and table is labelled with its method. Neither measures open-ended creativity.

The setup

I asked Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct to pick a random integer between 1 and 100. It put more than 95% of its probability on three numbers: 4, 42 and 47. The model is a mixture of experts: 30 billion parameters, about 3 billion active per token.

I trained it for 50 steps of GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization) on that one task, rewarding answers spread across 1 to 100. GRPO samples a group of answers per prompt and moves the model toward those that scored above the group average. The update is a small, published LoRA adapter and cost about $25 in Tinker compute. I then measured the trained task and nine untrained ones.

The exact prompt for one task, a flat string with no chat template:

System: You are a uniform random sampler. Output ONLY one card suit from the list below, exactly as written. No punctuation, no extra words.

Valid card suits: Spades, Hearts, Diamonds, Clubs
User: Pick a random card suit.
Assistant:

Each candidate, with a leading space, is scored as the text after Assistant:. Results are averaged over paraphrases of the user line, such as "Produce one random card suit."

Distance from uniform is the total variation (TV) distance to a uniform distribution: half the sum, over the K candidates, of |p − 1/K|. It is 0 when every candidate has the same probability and 1 − 1/K when one candidate has it all: 0.75 for four card suits, 0.999 for 1,000 integers.

Capability. I observed no decline on the capability subsets tested (200 MMLU questions, 50 GSM8K problems).

Second model family

Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct (dense, 8B), same 50-step recipe and candidate scoring: distance from uniform fell on 7 of 9 held-out tasks, and 5 of 9 cleared my 0.05 transfer threshold (mean held-out drop 0.105). The transfer concentrated on integers (integer 1 to 10: 0.527 to 0.116; integer 1 to 1000: 0.386 to 0.123). First name and card suit each got 0.008 worse. Capability subsets (MMLU n = 57, GSM8K n = 30) showed no observed decline. Full report.

Sampled answers

A user sees sampled text, not probabilities. On three tasks I sampled 50 answers per cell, with the chat template and two paraphrases. Outputs that do not parse to a listed candidate are dropped, and distance from uniform is computed over the valid outputs only.

Temperature above 1.0 gives less likely answers more weight. Raising the original model from T=1.0 to 1.5 moved all three tasks toward uniform, but each stayed further from uniform than the trained model at 1.0:

What 50 samples can show. Even a perfectly uniform sampler does not produce uniform counts. With 50 draws over 100 integers its distance from uniform cannot fall below 0.50 and averages (1 − 1/100)50 = 0.605. Trained integer 1 to 100 (0.600) and fruit (0.250) fall inside the uniform sampler's range: at this sample size the measurement is saturated and cannot tell the trained model from uniform. It does not show the model is uniform. Color (0.428) and every original-model cell are above the range. The sampling run was cut short, so the trained model was sampled on only these three tasks.

Interpretation and limits

Fifty GRPO steps on one random-integer task changed a measurable sampling behavior beyond that task. Under candidate scoring, all nine untrained tasks moved toward uniform on Qwen; on Llama the change stayed mostly within integers. How broad the change is, and whether it is useful, is not established.

Limits that affect the estimates above:

  • Candidate scoring ignores probability on answers outside the list. Uniform is the right target for integers or card suits, but not clearly for fruit or words.
  • Sampled numbers cover three tasks, two of them saturated at 50 samples (see Sampled answers).

Next experiment

  • The full sampled comparison: all ten tasks, both models, T=1.0 and 1.5, with sample counts set by each task's K or each cell compared with a matched finite-sample uniform reference. A flat 200 samples would not do: a uniform sampler over 1,000 integers still averages 0.819 at that count.
  • One prompt format (chat template or flat string) for both scoring and sampling, so a scored-versus-sampled gap is not confounded by a change in model input.
  • Untested behaviors: instruction following, calibration, safety, tool use, JSON formatting, full capability benchmarks, and free-form generation such as essays (an earlier embedding-based pilot on free text was inconclusive).
  • Inference-time baselines: contrastive decoding, base-model-assisted decoding, rejection sampling.
  • Other training tasks, model sizes and families.
  • Settings where a fixed candidate list matters in practice: multiple-choice evaluation with shuffled options, and synthetic-data generation that asks one model for many variants of an item.

Appendix: numeric-signature diversity on GSM8K

On a math problem the answer is fixed, so any variety is in the working. I sampled 10 solutions to each of 25 GSM8K problems (test items 0 to 24) under four conditions. For each correct solution, the numeric signature is every number in the body, sorted, excluding the final answer line. I counted distinct signatures among the correct solutions, so the count cannot exceed the number correct.

At T=1.0 the trained model had 1.04 more distinct signatures per problem than the original (95% CI +0.40 to +1.68, paired bootstrap over the 25 problems). It was higher on 13 problems, tied on 9 and lower on 3. Against the original at T=1.5 the difference was +0.04 (CI −0.60 to +0.60): on this measure, training and a higher temperature did about the same thing.

The signature is a crude proxy. Two real pairs of correct solutions show what it counts:

  • Same arithmetic, two signatures. On problem 0 (Janet's ducks), two original-model samples at T=1.0 both compute 3 + 4 = 7, 16 − 7 = 9 and 9 × $2 = $18. The second numbers 7 steps instead of 4 and restates some quantities a different number of times, so they count as two paths.
  • Different wording, one signature. On problem 1 (bolts of fiber), two trained-model samples write the halving step as \frac{1}{2} \times 2 = 1 and (1/2) × 2 = 1. They share a signature. A third trained sample does the same arithmetic but also writes "half of 2 bolts", and that extra 2 makes it a separate path.

So the count mixes layout with method: it splits solutions that differ only in step numbering or repeated givens, merges solutions that compute the same values in a different order, and says nothing about whether a path is better.
