Hidden-state probes outperform self-reported confidence

[ 2026-04-25 · updated 2026-09-23 ] // Llama 3.1 8B // 10 MIN READ

On Llama 3.1 8B, a linear probe on layer-19 hidden states ranks correct claims above incorrect ones better than the model's own stated confidence, in legal QA (MAUD) and in biographies (FActScore). Its Brier score improves only in the biography setting.

GitHub repo
TL;DR

I compared two per-claim scores from Llama 3.1 8B Instruct: its stated confidence, and a linear probe trained on its hidden states. AUROC measures how well a score ranks correct claims above incorrect ones, from 0.5 (chance) to 1.0 (perfect). On MAUD, where Llama answers questions about merger agreements, self-report scored 0.511 and the probe 0.771, a difference of 0.260 (95% interval 0.137 to 0.387). On FActScore, where Llama reads biographies written by ChatGPT, self-report scored 0.541 and the probe 0.802, a difference of 0.262 (0.180 to 0.332, resampling whole biographies). The probe beats a base-rate constant on Brier score on FActScore but not on MAUD.

Each setting has its own probe, so this is the method replicating, not one probe transferring. On a third dataset, FELM, the probe was ahead but its interval includes zero, and relabeling FELM showed its labels measured reference coverage, not correctness.

Setup

A long answer can be right in one sentence and wrong in the next, so the useful score is per claim. The cheapest one is to ask the model: I show Llama 3.1 8B Instruct one fixed claim and ask how confident it is, in a structured format. I call this self-report. Because the claim is given, a failure can't be blamed on vague claim boundaries.

The probe is deliberately plain. Llama passes a vector from each layer to the next, called the residual stream or hidden state. At layer 19, I average that vector over the claim's tokens, standardize it, and fit a logistic regression, with no feature search and no learned pooling.

01 · MAUD Legal QA, labels from model judges 150 claims taken from Llama's own answers to questions about merger agreements, drawn from 135 source examples. GPT-5.4 labels each claim true, partially_true or false. Kimi K2.6 relabels the same claims as a check.
02 · FActScore Biographies, human labels 157 biographies written by ChatGPT, with 4,886 atomic facts labeled by people. A fact almost never appears word for word, so I average over the sentence it came from.
03 · FELM-wk General knowledge, the hard case Human labels on segments of ChatGPT answers to general-knowledge questions. 71 test segments.

Labels are binary. On MAUD, only true counts as correct, and partially_true counts as incorrect. On FActScore, a fact is either supported or not, and facts marked irrelevant are dropped.

I fitted a separate probe in each setting. MAUD has too few claims to split, so I used leave-one-out: each claim is scored by a probe trained on the other 149. FActScore is split by biography into 3,353 training, 791 tuning and 742 test claims. The tuning set picks the regularization, and the test set is scored once.

Intervals are 95% paired bootstrap intervals, with every method scored on the same resample. FActScore's 742 test claims come from only 24 biographies, so there I resample whole biographies (10,000 resamples). Resampling single claims instead, also 10,000 times, gives a narrower interval on the difference: 0.217 to 0.304. MAUD and FELM intervals resample single claims (1,000 resamples).

Ranking and Brier score

AUROC only measures ranking. The Brier score measures how close predicted probabilities are to the 0/1 labels (lower is better). The fair reference is a constant that always predicts the base rate p, which scores p(1−p).

On the FActScore test set, 509 of 742 facts are true (68.6%). The base-rate constant scores 0.215, and always answering 0.5 scores 0.25. Self-report scores 0.298, worse than both. The probe scores 0.171, 0.044 below the constant. The interval on that difference is 0.009 to 0.075 with biographies resampled (0.024 to 0.063 with single claims resampled).

On MAUD, 62 of 150 claims are true (41%), so the base-rate constant scores 0.242. The probe scores 0.244, no better than the constant, and self-report scores 0.580.

Checking with a second judge

MAUD labels come from GPT-5.4, so a probe trained on them might be learning GPT-5.4's habits. I relabeled the same 150 claims with Kimi K2.6, using the same prompt, and did not refit the probe. Under Kimi labels, the probe scores 0.707 [0.627, 0.792] and self-report scores 0.466 [0.413, 0.604]. The paired difference is 0.241 [0.075, 0.320]. The ranking of methods did not change (appendix, Figure 2). One method beats the probe under both judges: asking GPT-5.4 directly to score each claim.

The two judges give the same three-way label to 110 of the 150 claims (Cohen's kappa 0.572, interval 0.456 to 0.682). On those 110, the probe scores 0.793 and self-report scores 0.486. This is not independent confirmation, because on those claims Kimi's labels match the labels the probe was trained on. The subset may also be easier: 27 of the 40 claims it drops were partially_true under GPT-5.4.

When the labels measure the wrong thing

FELM-wk is the warning case. On 71 test segments, the probe reached 0.652 AUROC and self-report 0.511. The difference, 0.141, has an interval of -0.063 to 0.320.

Then I tried to fix the labels. When Llama wrote its own answers to FELM questions, its claims often went beyond FELM's short reference texts. So I added fuller reference texts pulled from the source pages, plus a fourth label, not_enough_evidence. On a 52-segment trial, the labels came back like this:

A judge limited to the reference cannot decide a claim that goes beyond it, so the label records whether the reference covers the claim, not whether the claim is true. I did not scale up this FELM run.

Interpretation and limits

In these two settings, Llama 3.1 8B's layer-19 hidden states carry information about which claims are correct that its structured self-report does not surface, and a linear probe fitted on each setting's labels can read it out. The Brier score improves only on FActScore. The probe ranks claims; it is not a detector of legal or factual truth.

The FELM investigation exposed a label-design failure: reference coverage was being used as a substitute for correctness. That limits what can be learned from a probe trained on those labels. Before fitting a probe, it helps to know what the label measures and to pool activations over the text the label describes.

Issues that affect the current estimates:

  • MAUD evaluation is not grouped by source example. The 150 claims come from 135 examples, so leave-one-out can score a claim with a probe trained on a sibling claim, and the bootstrap resamples single claims. Grouped cross-validation is untested because the per-claim MAUD features are not committed to the repo.
  • MAUD labels come from model judges, not lawyers. I set aside 30 claims for expert review, but they are not labeled yet.
  • On FActScore, Llama reads text that ChatGPT wrote, so this tests how Llama represents text it didn't write, not its uncertainty about its own output. Activations cover the fact's parent sentence, not the fact itself.

Scope: one model and one layer. Every probe uses layer 19 of Llama 3.1 8B Instruct, chosen because the Goodfire SAE used for comparison is trained there. No probe fitted on one domain was scored on the other. A fourth dataset, CUAD, failed at the claim-extraction step.

Related work

  • Verbal uncertainty. Lin, Hilton and Evans (2022) trained models to express calibrated uncertainty in words. Kadavath et al. (2022) found that models can often evaluate their own answers in the right format.
  • Truthfulness probes. Azaria and Mitchell (2023) trained classifiers on hidden activations to predict whether statements are true, Burns et al. (2023) looked for latent knowledge without supervision, and Marks and Tegmark (2024) found linear structure for true and false statements. Those works mostly use controlled statement datasets; I apply a linear readout to claims from longer answers.
  • Semantic entropy. Farquhar et al. (2024) estimate uncertainty over the meanings of sampled generations. Kossen et al. (2024) approximate that from the hidden states of a single generation. I predict the correctness of a fixed claim instead.
  • Hallucination detection. Obeso et al. (2025) train probes that flag hallucinated entities token by token in long-form generation, across several model families. This work is claim-level.

ProbeMon

The repo includes ProbeMon, research software that packages the method: two pretrained layer-19 probes for Llama 3.1 8B Instruct (legal-qa-llama-3.1-8b-v1 from MAUD, biography-llama-3.1-8b-v1 from FActScore), a fit_probe function for your own labeled claims, loaders for the two datasets, and a command-line tool (probemon list-probes, score, train). It refuses to score a model that doesn't match the probe.

Does the probe break down into features?

No. Projecting the MAUD probe direction onto the Goodfire layer-19 SAE features gives about the same concentration as random directions do. The signal is spread across many directions (probe_interpretation.md). The probe is a useful readout, not an explanation of how the model represents correctness.
