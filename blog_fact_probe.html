A probe on Llama’s hidden states beats its stated confidence, and the signal is spread across the network

[ 2026-10-18 ] // Llama 3.1 8B // 13 MIN READ

In April I reported that a linear probe on Llama 3.1 8B’s hidden states ranks factual claims better than the model’s own stated confidence. This follow-up tests that result against a stronger way of reading the model’s confidence and against two confounds, then asks, in a preregistered analysis, where in the network the probe’s signal comes from. The data behind it can be explored in an interactive page.

TL;DR

On 742 held-out claims from 24 FActScore biographies, the probe reaches AUROC 0.802. Llama’s stated probability reaches 0.541, because it gives exactly 1.0 to 650 of the 742 claims. The probe’s lead over the stated probability holds when only claims about the same person are compared, which keeps subject popularity fixed. Reading the probability that Llama answers “True” about one claim at a time (P(True)) is a much stronger baseline, at 0.735, and in an exploratory test a two-weight combination of the probe and P(True) beats both. Removing what sentence position and year mentions explain leaves 0.645 within biographies, cross-fitted over all 157, which passed a 0.60 bar set before the analysis was run.

A preregistered, exact decomposition puts 79% of the position-residualized probe’s within-biography signal in attention heads. The neuron part is dense: the 50 most important of 286,720 neurons hold 1.25% of the neurons’ positive contribution. Knocking out the top 200 neurons hurt the probe more than knocking out random ones, but by 0.026, short of the preregistered 0.03. An LLM rater found the top neurons legible, and no more so than matched random neurons.

Status

The decomposition, the knockout test and the neuron ratings were preregistered. The plan, its first three amendments and the plans for the exploratory analyses were each committed before the data they cover; a fourth amendment records a data error found after all results. The earlier comparisons with the stated probability, P(True), popularity and position were not part of the preregistration. A workshop paper is in draft and not peer reviewed. The code and results are on a branch of scasella/activation-probes-claim-correctness that is not public yet.

Setup

FActScore (Min et al., 2023) has 157 biographies written by ChatGPT, split into 4,886 atomic facts that annotators marked as supported or not. Llama 3.1 8B Instruct reads each biography. For each fact, I average Llama’s layer-19 residual stream over the fact’s source sentence and fit an L2 logistic regression on 133 biographies. The probe is then scored once on the 24 held-out biographies, 742 facts, of which 233 are wrong.

I compare it with two ways of reading Llama’s own confidence:

  • Stated probability. Llama sees the biography and the facts, in chunks of up to 20, and gives a probability that each is correct. In April I called this self-report and described it as one claim at a time; on FActScore it actually saw the whole biography and up to 20 claims at once. Llama did not write these biographies, so this measures Llama verifying someone else’s claims. The explorer calls it Llama-as-verifier.
  • P(True). Llama sees one fact at a time, with the subject named, and I read the probability that its first answer token is “True”.

The scores are AUROC: the chance that a randomly chosen correct claim is scored above a randomly chosen incorrect one, where 0.5 is a coin flip and 1.0 is perfect. Within-biography AUROC compares only claims about the same person. Brackets are 95% bootstrap intervals, resampling claims or whole biographies as stated.

This is not the first probe of its kind. Han et al. (2025) trained claim-level probes on Llama hidden states, tested them on FActScore and compared them with verbalized confidence and P(True). What this study adds is a paired comparison with stated and token-probability confidence on human labels, popularity and position controls, the probe–P(True) combination, and the decomposition.

Position is a confound too, and the signal survives it

Later sentences in a biography are wrong more often, and the probe partly encodes that. Cross-fitted over all 157 biographies in five folds, position alone reaches 0.654 within biographies, against 0.689 for the probe. A gate fixed before it was run removed the probe’s linear dependence on sentence position and year mentions, using slopes fitted on the other folds. The residualized score still reaches 0.645 [0.614, 0.675], above the gate’s 0.60 bar, and passing it licensed the preregistered analysis below. This is the claim-level signal I can defend, and the score that the decomposition and the knockouts below take apart.

Two signals beat one (exploratory)

P(True) closes most of the stated probability’s gap. The probe leads it across the test set, +0.067 [0.003, 0.124] with biographies resampled, but not within biographies over all 157 (+0.029 [−0.009, 0.068]). The two scores are only weakly correlated (0.35), so I tested a linear stack of the two, with two weights fitted within biographies on the training folds.

Within biographies over all 157, the stack reaches 0.745 [0.719, 0.769], against 0.689 for the probe and 0.660 for P(True). That is +0.055 [0.039, 0.072] over the probe and +0.085 [0.056, 0.113] over P(True). Claims from one sentence share the probe’s score, so part of the gain is the stack breaking those ties. Leaving out same-sentence pairs, the gain over the probe is +0.042 [0.025, 0.059]. On the test split the stack reaches 0.834 against the probe’s 0.802. I planned this test after the single-score results were known, and committed the plan before computing any combined score.

Where the signal lives

The probe reads one direction in Llama’s layer-19 residual stream. Everything in that stream was written there by an attention head, an MLP neuron or the token embedding, so the probe’s score splits exactly into a direct contribution from each of 286,720 neurons, 640 heads and the embedding. I measured each component’s share of the residualized probe’s within-biography separation between correct and incorrect claims. I wanted to test a claim from Cammarata’s essay “Neural Networks Are Already Clean”, that probe-like directions break down into a few readable neurons.

The heads’ part is somewhat concentrated: the strongest head carries 5.0% of the separation and the top ten 33%. The neurons’ part is not. Neurons push hard in both directions and nearly cancel. Knocking components out

If a set of components carries the signal, replacing their output with its average (mean ablation) should hurt the probe more than doing the same to random components from the same layers. Random neurons were also matched on how much their contribution varies; random heads only on layer. Scores here are within-biography AUROCs of the residualized probe, which starts at 0.644 (the fp32 counterpart of the gate’s 0.645).

The preregistered test knocked out the 200 neurons with the most held-out mass. That cost 0.032 [0.016, 0.046] of AUROC, against 0.006 for matched random neurons. The difference, 0.026 [0.013, 0.038], is clearly above zero but short of the 0.03 I set in advance, so by the preregistered rule these neurons are not necessary.

Exploratory follow-ups, each planned before its data: E1 knocked out the top 10 heads, and E2 extended both curves. Cost grows steadily with set size, from 0.019 for the top 10 heads to 0.213 [0.179, 0.250] for the top 160 heads and 0.219 [0.180, 0.259] for the top 5,000 neurons. Random sets, averaged over three per size, never cost more than 0.010. At 20,000 neurons they slightly raise the score (−0.019), so top − random there overstates the top set's cost.

At the largest sizes the residualized score falls below chance (0.425, 0.376 and 0.431 after 5,000 neurons, 20,000 neurons and 160 heads). On the raw probe logit, which starts at 0.689, the same knockouts leave 0.485, 0.406 and 0.534, so only the 20,000-neuron set is clearly below chance on both scales. Why is untested: the selection took only components with positive mass, so those pushing the wrong way stay in place, and the residualized score subtracts fixed position slopes. Read these points as showing dependence, not measuring it.

The top neurons read like random ones

For the legibility test I took the 100 carriers, the 50 neurons with the most positive and the 50 with the most negative mass. I paired each with a random neuron from the same layer whose contribution varies about as much, drawn from outside the top 1,000. A rater saw the 200 in shuffled order, with no layer, mass or role. For each neuron it saw the 10 highest- and 10 lowest-activating biography sentences, shaded token by token, and the highest- and lowest-activating web-text contexts from a FineWeb sample. It wrote a description, judged whether the description fits at least 14 of the 20 sentences, and labelled the neuron as tracking knowledge (epistemic), a confound such as topic or format, or a mix.

Before any rating existed, I made an LLM (Claude Opus 5.5) the rater of record instead of myself, in a dated amendment. That brings back the circularity raised against the essay, whose neuron explanations are also written and judged by models. A second, independent pass of the same model agreed with κ 0.83 on fit and 0.76 on category, which shows consistency, not validity.

  • Nearly everything is legible. 98 of 100 carriers and 95 of 100 random neurons got a description that fits, so the test sits near its ceiling.
  • Confound-led, like random neurons. Weighted by mass, the coherent carriers are 90% confound, 9% mixed and 1.2% epistemic. Confound labels are no more common among carriers than among random neurons (87 of 98 against 91 of 95, Fisher p = 0.10). The rubric counts a claim type or topic, such as awards or family, as a confound, so both groups come out confound-heavy. The label reflects the rubric as much as the neurons.
  • Descriptions predict carriers no better. In an exploratory test, a second model (gpt-6.1-sol) used each description to predict the neuron’s activations on held-out sentences. Spearman correlations were 0.33 for carriers and 0.27 for random neurons, a difference of 0.062 [−0.014, 0.137] with neuron pairs resampled. No human wrote or checked either side, and the control that would set a chance level was not run.
  • Activation is not effect. A neuron’s contribution is its activation times its readout. For 32 of the 50 positive carriers, higher activation lowers the probe’s score; they rank correct claims higher by being less active on them. Eight carriers change readout sign across folds. The rater saw activation only, so its descriptions say what a neuron fires on, not how firing moves the score.

Legible, and read like random neurons

98 of 100 carriers and 95 of 100 random neurons got a description that fits. Weighted by mass, the coherent carriers are 90% confound, 9% mixed and 1.2% epistemic: Primary A reads CONFOUND-LED. Confound features are no more common among carriers than among random neurons (87/98 against 91/95, Fisher p = 0.10). The preregistered rubric counts a claim type or topic (awards, education, family) as a confound, so both groups come out confound-heavy: the label reflects the rubric as much as the carriers, and the comparison with random neurons is the informative one.

Primary B asks whether the top carriers are as legible as Cammarata's essay “Neural Networks Are Already Clean” predicts. It reads ESSAY-CONSISTENT for the top carriers of a dense probe, on a test near its ceiling: 2% of carriers got a description that did not fit their sentences, against 5% of random neurons. But the 50 positive carriers hold only 1.25% of the positive neuron mass, and neurons carry 21% of the total separation.

Explore it

The Fact Probe Explorer holds the data behind this post. You can read any of the 24 test biographies and flag the claims each signal trusts least, to see which errors it catches. It shows how many errors each signal catches as more claims are flagged, every claim plotted by probe score against P(True), and a grid of all 640 heads. All 286,720 neurons are drawn one pixel each, with a slider that lights up the top N. There are the knockout curves, and a browser for the 200 rated neurons, with each neuron’s sentences, web-text contexts and description. Every number on the page is read from the study’s result files, and a sources table lists the file and key for each one.

The public explorer leaves out which of the 200 neurons were carriers, along with their layers, masses and readouts, so the same packet can still be rated blind by people.

Interpretation and limits

The positive result is that a simple probe on Llama’s hidden states ranks claims much better than Llama’s stated confidence. The lead holds up when popularity and position are controlled, and the probe adds to P(True), the strongest read of Llama’s own confidence I tested. What the probe reads is distributed: most of it comes through attention heads, and the neuron part is spread over tens of thousands of neurons that mostly cancel. I found no small, readable set of neurons that carries it.

  • One model, one layer and one pooling choice. Llama 3.1 8B Instruct, layer 19 and sentence pooling were fixed in advance, so facts from one sentence share a score.
  • The test split is small: 24 biographies. The probe scored 0.728 on validation and 0.802 on test, and with whole test biographies resampled its interval is [0.748, 0.848].
  • Popularity explains much of the pooled score. The within-biography comparisons are the ones that hold it fixed.
  • The stated probability is Llama verifying text ChatGPT wrote. P(True) is the stronger read of its confidence, and sampled self-consistency is still untested.
  • The decomposition counts direct effects only. Each component is credited with what it writes straight into the layer-19 stream; influence that passes through later components is credited to those components.
  • The preregistered knockout test missed its bar. The stack, the head knockout, the dose-response curves and the simulation test are exploratory. Each was chosen after related results were known, then fixed in a dated plan before its own data existed.
  • Legibility was judged by an LLM, near the coherence ceiling and under a rubric that files topics as confounds.
  • In one training biography, a decoding clean-up made three sentence windows one token too long, which affects 12 of the 4,886 facts. Refitting the probes moves the test AUROC and the 0.645 by under 0.001. With the probes held fixed, no within-biography AUROC can move by more than 0.0055. The error is recorded as a dated amendment.

Next experiment

  • Human ratings of the same packet, by raters who haven’t seen which neurons are carriers.
  • A probe trained to ignore position, instead of a residualized score, and one trained within biographies so it can’t lean on subject popularity.
  • Sampled self-consistency and a semantic-entropy-probe baseline.
