A diversity LoRA transfers to synthetic-data generation

The same 50-step LoRA from the random-number experiment, tested on open-ended text generation. Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct mode-collapses on these prompts, repeating one synthetic math problem 46 times in 300 samples; the LoRA cuts exact duplicates from 67% to 27% and produces more semantically varied data than raising temperature.

GitHub repo LoRA adapter Part 1: answer diversity
TL;DR

An earlier post showed that 50 RL steps on one task ("pick a random integer 1–100") restored answer diversity across nine other categorical tasks. The open question was whether that transfers to open-ended free-text generation, the kind you run to build synthetic training data. It does, where it is needed.

Ask the vanilla model for 300 elementary math word problems and 67% come back exact duplicates; it writes "Sarah has 12 apples…" 46 times. The trained model cuts exact duplicates to 27%, more than triples the usable-unique yield, and produces more semantically varied problems than the same model sampled at temperature 1.3. The LoRA helps most where the model collapses: on a generator that was already diverse (fictional personas), there is nothing to fix.

Why this matters

Synthetic data is generated text used to train another model. The bottleneck is diversity. A generator that emits the same example hundreds of times wastes the budget you spent producing it and trains a narrower student. Deduplication after the fact only tells you how much you wasted; it does not give the variety back.

Instruction tuning makes the problem worse. The "make this model helpful" pass narrows the output distribution, the same narrowing the first post measured on categorical prompts. The natural worry is that a fix trained on "pick a random integer" helps only on toy categorical tasks and does nothing for real generation, where there is no fixed answer set to flatten.

The first post left this open: sentence-embedding distances saturate on short categorical outputs, so the free-text case stayed inconclusive. This post measures it on longer generations.

Terms used below
  • The LoRA. A rank-16 adapter trained with 50 GRPO steps on Qwen3-30B-A3B-Instruct, rewarding spread-out answers on the single task "pick a random integer 1–100." About $25 of compute. Same adapter as the first post; I retrained nothing for this one.
  • Mode collapse. The model places most of its probability on a few outputs, so repeated sampling returns near-identical text.
  • Exact-duplicate rate. Fraction of the 300 generations that are byte-identical to another after normalizing whitespace and case. Lower is more diverse.
  • Embedding cosine distance. Mean pairwise 1 − cos of the generations in all-MiniLM-L6-v2 space. Measures semantic spread, not surface wording. Higher is more diverse.
  • Unique-yield. Fraction of 300 generations left after greedy near-duplicate removal at cosine 0.9. "How many usable-distinct items did this budget buy." Higher is more diverse.
  • Temperature. The standard sampling diversity knob. T=1.3 is the control: if the LoRA only does what hotter sampling does, it is not worth a forked model.

Setup

One model, one adapter, three generators, three sampling conditions. The generators are short prompts I asked the model to answer 300 times each, the kind of fixed prompt a synthetic-data pipeline loops on:

  • Math word problem. "Write one elementary-school math word problem."
  • Support question. "Write one customer-support question a user might ask about a SaaS product."
  • Character persona. "Write a two-sentence description of a fictional character."

The three conditions are the vanilla model at temperature 1.0, the vanilla model at temperature 1.3 (the diversity control), and the trained model at temperature 1.0. Each cell is 300 samples drawn from the fixed prompt; diversity comes only from sampling, which is the property the LoRA changes. I take the first item from each generation, so a model that rambles past one problem is not credited for the ramble. I ran sampling through Tinker and computed the metrics locally.

The headline: where the model repeats itself

The vanilla model recites math problems more than it generates them. Of 300 requests it returns 100 distinct problems, and a single template, "Sarah has 12 apples. She gives 5 to her friend and buys 8 more," accounts for 46 of them. The trained model returns 219 distinct problems from the same 300 requests, and its most frequent item appears 9 times. The LoRA beats temperature

Raising temperature to 1.3 is the cheap fix and it helps, but on the math generator it does not catch the LoRA. The trained model is more semantically diverse and yields more unique problems than the hotter vanilla model, and the gaps survive a bootstrap. Resampling the 300 items 400 times, every trained-minus-baseline interval excludes zero.

Temperature and the LoRA do different things. Temperature jitters word choice, which lifts surface metrics. The LoRA moves the model to different problems, which cosine distance and unique-yield pick up. On the math generator the second effect is larger, and it stacks past what temperature reaches.

Where it helps, and where it does not

The effect tracks how collapsed the generator was to begin with. The chart below plots usable-yield across all three generators: how concentrated each model is, and how much the LoRA spreads it out.

Three regimes. The math generator is where the model collapses, and the LoRA helps most there, beating temperature. On support questions the collapse is milder, the trained model beats vanilla at the same temperature, and raising temperature is competitive. On personas the model is already diverse at temperature 1.0, every condition lands near a unique-yield of 1.0, and the trained model edges the others only on semantic spread. The LoRA undoes collapse, and does little where there is no collapse to undo.

Does the diversity buy anything?

Diversity is only worth measuring if it has downstream value. The cheapest test without training a student: how well does each synthetic corpus cover the real distribution it is meant to stand in for. I embedded 300 GSM8K-train questions and, for each, found its nearest neighbor in each synthetic math corpus.

The trained corpus covers the real distribution best, ahead of both vanilla conditions. The absolute coverage is low for every condition: a 30B instruct model asked for one-line grade-school problems writes easier, more templated problems than GSM8K regardless of diversity. Diversity changes the ranking, not the absolute level.

Bounds

This is one adapter on one model, measured on three generators I chose. Replicate with the model and the prompts you generate from before trusting it.

The win is conditional on collapse. The headline holds on the math generator because the vanilla model collapses there; it shrinks to a same-temperature-only edge on support questions and vanishes on personas. A reader who needs diverse personas gets nothing from this. The LoRA undoes collapse; it does not raise diversity everywhere.

Diversity is not quality. Every metric here measures spread, not whether the generated problems are good, solvable, or well-formed. I read samples and the trained problems are coherent and varied, but I did not grade them, and I did not run the stronger test: train a student model on the vanilla and trained corpora and compare it on a held-out task. The GSM8K-coverage number is a proxy for downstream value, not a downstream result.

The trained model is more verbose. It runs past "write one problem" more often than vanilla and gets truncated at the token cap more often. First-item extraction removes the tail before it reaches any metric, so the diversity numbers are clean, but the verbosity is a real behavior change and would cost more tokens per usable item in production.

Close

The first post showed a small RL pass restores answer diversity on categorical tasks and that the effect transfers across tasks the model never trained on. The open question was whether "diversity" meant anything beyond flattening a fixed answer set. On the generator where it matters, it does. The same adapter cuts duplicate synthetic math problems from two-thirds to one-quarter, produces semantically more varied data than turning temperature up, and covers the real distribution better, all from a $25 training run done for a different task.

Two things would turn this from a measurement into a tool: a student trained on the diverse corpus, and a model that does not pay for the diversity in extra tokens. Until then the bounded claim stands. If your synthetic-data generator repeats itself, an off-task diversity adapter is one cheap way to make it stop, and temperature is not a full substitute.
